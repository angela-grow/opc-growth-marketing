## Why it works

People watching a software ad are trying to answer one question: what does this thing actually do? Most ads fail that test in one of two ways. They show too much, like a dashboard full of tiny tabs, and the viewer's brain gives up. Or they show too little, a voiceover describing a feature you cannot see. Showing one feature at a time keeps the load low enough that the message lands.

Most people will skip your ad, even a good one. If the brand name appears in the first five seconds, even a skipped ad leaves a trace. Repeated over many impressions, that trace becomes familiarity.

Vague outcomes like "save time" or "increase revenue" create no picture in the mind. A specific moment does: "My CFO gets mad after seeing I went over budget on travel." Concrete scenes are easier to understand and far easier to recall.

Finally, the ad that looks different is the one people remember. An exaggerated prop or a fast cut gives the eye something unexpected, which holds attention.

## Steps

1. **Start from a specific, human problem.** Good hooks are specific, interpersonal and emotional. "Lately, it seems your career depends on how fast you adopt AI" beats "Everyone's working harder. Not enough are working smarter." Let customers' own words write your hooks.
2. **Show the product, one feature per 30 seconds.** Strip each process down to an input and an output. Give away completeness for clarity. Zoom in on the one part of the screen that matters.
3. **Trigger it with a real job to be done.** Use a real task the viewer needs to get done as the bridge between their pain and the product.
4. **Say the brand name in the first five seconds,** and show it on screen. Test it: play only the first five seconds to a stranger. They should know what the ad is for.
5. **Show moments, not outcomes.** Pick a scene the viewer can picture: where they feel the benefit and what they are doing. Show the immediate benefit first, then link it to the bigger promise. Make it relational: who else is happier because the problem got solved?
6. **Make it native to each format.** Never copy and paste one ad across channels. LinkedIn: vertical and creator-led, or horizontal with good production. CTV: horizontal, very polished. YouTube pre-roll: horizontal and polished; Shorts: vertical and creator-led. Meta: vertical and creator-led.
7. **Add something visually striking.** A bold prop, three quick shots in the first five seconds, or strong visual effects. These were the three traits Google found in YouTube ads that hold attention.

## Example

Connor Lewis has made more than 852 B2B video ads for companies including ClickUp, Notion, Fingerprint and Zapier. For Scribe, whose buyers' pain is out-of-date onboarding material, the ad used a dusty binder labelled "manuals from 1999" as its prop: a pain point you can hold, instantly recognisable and exaggerated in one dimension.

## Prerequisites

- Real customer language for hooks, from sales calls or interviews.
- Budget for production, especially for CTV and YouTube.
- A target audience, so the ads reach buyers rather than everyone.

## How to measure

- Hook rate and view-through rate in the first five seconds.
- Average watch time compared with your other ads.
- Ad recall or brand lift where platforms offer it.

## Pitfalls

- Showing the whole product in one ad.
- Hiding the brand until the end, where most viewers never get to.
- Promising outcomes ("ROI!") with no scene to make them real.
- Reusing a LinkedIn ad on CTV or Meta without re-editing it.
