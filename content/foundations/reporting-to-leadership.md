Attribution will never be perfect, but that is no excuse for missing the fundamentals. Your job is to track the right things, make sense of them for leadership, and be ready when the CFO asks "can you just double MQLs (marketing-qualified leads) next quarter?" or the CEO asks "how did we beat our pipeline target?". This guide comes from Eric MacColl, former head of enterprise demand generation at Atlassian. For the three ways to judge whether a tactic is working, read [Measuring attribution, influence and incrementality](#foundation-measuring-impact).

## Set the data foundation

The 80/20 that most reporting depends on:

- **Web analytics and event tracking.** Analytics set up properly, conversion events tracked in each ad platform, one standard naming convention for UTM tags, and UTM fields stored in your marketing automation platform and CRM.
- **Lifecycle stages defined and tracked.** Date stamps when contacts and accounts enter each stage, definitions agreed by sales and marketing, and clear service levels for lead follow-up.
- **Campaign records in the CRM** for each channel and asset, with custom fields for source and UTMs.
- **Contacts always added to opportunities.** Work with sales operations so every opportunity has at least one primary contact. Without this, marketing's influence on deals is invisible.

## Structure the quarterly business review

Three sections:

1. **What:** the current state. Pacing against plan for the metrics that matter, such as marketing-sourced opportunities and qualified leads.
2. **Why:** insights and learnings. Channel deep dives (spend, cost per qualified contact, pipeline, quarter on quarter), top-performing assets, and account engagement for ABM programmes.
3. **So what:** the marketing roadmap. Which campaigns run next, by month and channel.

## Agree the principles before the numbers

The hardest part is often the people you report to, not the tracking. Share these principles with your leadership and ask what they agree or disagree with:

- **Attribution is a compass, not a calculator.** It shows direction, not truth. Use it to learn, not to prove. The goal is less uncertainty, not perfect prediction.
- **Be open about your model's assumptions.** What counts as a touch, the lookback window, how credit is weighted. Touchpoint attribution over-counts clickable events and cannot see awareness.
- **Treat attribution as evidence, not proof.** If sponsored webinars show up in winning deals, that is a signal, not causation. Confirm patterns from several angles: deal stories, multi-touch models, A/B tests, incrementality tests and buyer surveys.
- **One team, one goal.** Attribution should not be a weapon for defending budgets. Align teams on shared measures such as pipeline, win rate and account progression.

## Answers to the questions boards ask

**"What's the ROI of our LinkedIn ads?"** Show a real customer journey. One example account had 41 touchpoints across 17 people before converting: webinars, content syndication, marketing and outbound emails, a demo request and partner marketing. Giving all the credit to the first or last touch makes no sense.

**"Is our ABM programme actually making a difference?"** Compare segments of accounts against a control group. In one example, accounts in the ABM programme saw a 75% increase in monthly opportunity creation against 12% for other mid-market accounts. A high-engagement ABM segment saw a 77% increase in opportunity creation during the pilot.

**"Can we just triple MQLs next quarter?"** Split the funnel by source. You can triple leads, just not the leads you want. In one example, demo requests converted from marketing-qualified lead to sales-qualified lead at 45% and chat at 100%, while sponsored trade shows converted at 2% and ebooks at 5%. Not all leads are created equal.
